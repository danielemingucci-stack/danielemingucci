It is not a question of being against or in favour, but of understanding how this tool will enter our lives and our relationships. Basically, I do not think it will be Europeans who make incredible fortunes, but certainly - as happens with social platforms - we will have at our disposal a series of tools useful for making money and for improving the quality of marketing, opening up new market opportunities.

Upstream of all this, though, there are aspects on which we are probably deluding ourselves. The first is that it is a tool that prevents the creation of monopolies. The NFT introduces rarity into the digital world, creating originals and copies. The bone has been thrown to the digital community to see whether and how money can be made, a flood of ideas has started up (I have at least a couple myself) and the initiated have begun pouring resources and talent into it, hoping to be chosen to become “the right ones”, perhaps the new Zucks. I am being a little ironic, it is true, and that is because it seems to me a swindler’s logic to exploit the epic of “they were born in a garage” in order to put to work millions of brains who will be left the crumbs. But moving on, the theme remains: how do you make money? There are no direct answers, but there are some successful case histories: the punks, the NBA and so on. Art, trading cards… collecting. Other cases closer to home are the monetisation done by Montemagno, who does not do it for money, he says, but in fact sells access (ten thousand passes) to a private room of his community at 0.08 ethereum, which is a lot of money. If the tool is new, the marketing mechanics do not seem all that new to me: art, collecting, exclusive access… the logic of the marketplace remains, and here whoever has more media power, more power to create consensus or community, objectively has the same kind of advantage as someone who can afford a shop in the centre in the right position.

Another theme, usability. The initiated are teaching us our letters - God bless them - on how wallets, markets, blockchains, transactions and so on work. And God really bless them, because the usability is a nightmare. You think you have understood, you feel you are in control, and in a flash, instead, you may be out of everything… Joking aside, we are light years from the comfort of “recover password”, data kept in the cloud and so on. You have to have super-secure access keys, you cannot trust leaving them on unprotected devices, connections are a total risk, and all because anonymity, non-recognisability, comes at a steep price. A parallel to make myself clear: in the Middle Ages whoever held the pouch of gold coins was the owner, and if it happened to be stolen from you it was lost (besides the fact that nobody knew how much was really in it, whether the money was real and so on). The world we are used to today is objectively much friendlier and more protective. Anonymity costs dearly and exposes you to very high risks. It is certainly an absolute pain in the neck: to get into important wallets securely you have to adapt to sorcerers’ liturgies. I do not like it.

I close with a theoretical doubt about the blockchain. I have to say it partly fascinates me and I partly detest it. It consumes a heap of time and energy, and that would bother me even if it did not pollute. Time is precious, not because it is money, but because it is life. Our miners’ and minters’ computers are throwing energy of every kind into building this interminable chain that protects transactions, true, but adds no quality. It seems to me a chain for slaves of security. But the other theme, the real doubt, is about the formulation at the base of the blockchain. The blockchain is based on consensus… well, that consensus can be bought is something history has shown several times. Will 51% of the blockchains really never end up in the hands of a single party? Let us hope so. In any case, today we pay a toll on every transaction: money (real money) or machine time.

These are thoughts lined up without having understood a heap of things, so I am surely wrong. But if I am wrong I am glad. The aim is absolutely not to be in favour or against, obviously (even if I am always pro), because these are tools we are already immersed in and which already offer great opportunities today. But I would like to frame it in the right perspective: in the ‘90s I used to connect to guest.net (or something like it), which believed in the internet many years before everyone else. I do not think it became a unicorn just for having arrived before the others… Taking off the table the illusion of being the new Zuckerbergs and of being in the right place to become them may perhaps help us find a more realistic dimension in which to present an offer to our markets, to the companies of our area or of our “circle”, without always finding ourselves two steps behind whatever the digital giants impose on us (see the algorithms of Google or Facebook, where we take the instructions and make them work, but have no active role, ever).
