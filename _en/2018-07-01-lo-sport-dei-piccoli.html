Coming to youth sport as a dad, in less than ten years I have seen it all. Right up to yesterday’s apotheosis, when a five-foot nonentity, self-proclaimed prophet of youth sport here where I live, attacked me for almost an hour with barely veiled threats, slander and intimidation of various kinds. He was hoping to make me lose my temper and he did not succeed because, unbeknown to him, I was living through a cathartic moment, one of those in which many confused thoughts slowly connect and suddenly - it happened late in the evening - take a sensible shape. The dots joined up.

The subject was youth sport: three quarters of my sons’ team give up just when it is getting good. We parents’ explanation is that there is too much pressure; the coaches’ is that the kids “must” show some guts. It is nothing new, and anyone in sport knows it well: at about 15, kids often, very often, give up. Can something be done? They “must” show some guts. But sports clubs, coaches, parents, friends, grandparents… can anyone make a contribution of some kind? No, because “they” must do it. Niceties aside, that is how the discussion ended.

The kids grow and - rightly - the coaches demand more, more, more. Those are the rules of competition, they say, and the kids “must” adapt. Only the kids have no wish at all for that hell of stress, pressure, repressed anger, tellings-off - three hours a day, five or six days a week. And they give up. We parents, I at least, have to send them to school, make them study, keep them away from bad company, give them timetables and so on. Do we also have to force them to do sport? Of course sport is important, but should it not also be recreation? In short, when they reach a certain age and other commitments begin to appear, if they decide to give up, they give up.

With the coaches there is no room for negotiation: the kids “must”, otherwise - expressed in more or less kind words - they are no use to us. If you go up a floor and turn to the directors there is an expert sense of impotence: that is how things are, the coaches cannot do more, they put in commitment, dedication and so on and so forth, and if the kids cannot take it, it is a pity but…

In short, there is nothing to be done! There are no values that can be brought into play, no dialogue, no mediation: I took an hour of insults for nothing, because nothing can be done except fail!

Sport is for the little ones and for a few scattered survivors (those who have better parents than me and than those who let their children give up).

But is it really all right like this? In practice, the ball is left entirely in the hands of the kids and their uncertain will. Yes, it is a way of selecting, but it is a selection so wide that it does raise some doubts. Here where I live, for example, despite what are perhaps the best facilities in Europe, there seems to be a trap that the over-15s do not survive: up to a certain point teams reach regional levels and beyond, then every two or three years everything is reset and people settle for mediocrity. Do coaches and directors really have a clear conscience? Can really nothing more be done? Do we parents (inexpert, by all means) really deserve to be attacked by moral dwarfs?

Italian sport dies young; it dies in the small towns and the suburbs where nobody seems able to bring positive values into play, where people cannot relate to one another without feuds threats slander intimidation, where the small aspirations of those in charge make the people small too: the great potential of our kids is defused before it can express itself. Perhaps because the champion is hoped for with such performance anxiety that nobody is given time to grow; perhaps adult frustrations are thrown onto the field, which the young gladly steer clear of; perhaps the work is simply done badly, without considering people - big and small - in their entirety, and everyone is content to count the participants without caring who they are. And what is lost is enthusiasm and people, not just matches…

It is a pot in which nothing is boiling, it rings hollow, and our kids go - rightly - elsewhere. Half-men, even if they find a setting in which to bawl and give orders, remain half-men in a mediocre world. A pity!
