I would like to write a book about Dante and have it begin like this:

Dante was born in Bologna, nobody quite knows when. He was probably already about twenty, and somehow he got his hands on the poetry of Guido Guinizzelli, his father [Purgatorio XXIV], from whom he received his definitive imprinting: Al cor gentil rempaira SEMPRE amore, to the gentle heart love ALWAYS returns…

In human development, the mother has the task of passing on to you the sense of being unique and extraordinary; the father, instead, the law, the order of things. Which is anything but a limit, because it is the secret of relation, of the link, of construction. They are the two legs we all walk on, identity and relation, and Dante received one of the two from Guido Guinizzelli. Yes, it is important to take history into account, the information, the annals and the attestations, but within all this sea of news and information, witnesses and protagonists must also be given their due weight. And the protagonist of this story says it clearly: my father is him, Guido Guinizzelli. The blood that flows in me is the same that flows in his veins. He says father, he does not say master.

He did not teach him to write or to speak, nor even to put thoughts into rhyme. The dolce stil novo is on another level: it is gratitude (where else would the sweetness come from, from simpering?), it is an attitude towards life, it is a way of looking at reality, an approach to what one has before one. And it is a novelty, a piece of news, something that happens. It is not the simple opposite of old or traditional: it is new! Boom! Everything is ordered to this: subjects, commitment, writing… These are the rules, the law he received from his father, this the warmth he felt flowing inside him. And so he set off.

The subject was serious, deadly serious: Love (capital or lower case, damn it?). Before going in, I think it is important to accept that the method of knowing has to be adapted to the thing in front of us: Dante tells his story, and then re-tells it and re-tells it again. He wants us to listen to him, and he also teaches us how he is to be interpreted. When he speaks of the four meanings of the scriptures, he makes a progression: the literal (easy, a bit of paraphrase is enough), the allegorical (the footnotes are more than sufficient), the moral (here we get intimidated, but it is only an invitation to look up, to make a link between the small and the infinitely great - which is simply to trust the heart) and the a-na-go-gi-cal (was it third or fourth?). Well, anagogical is what it has to do with the highest things, with the great systems, with the absolutes. Another invitation to a link, to a relationship, to put oneself in the middle and look up.

The spirit is that of finding a way to build a relation between oneself and the infinite, between oneself and destiny. This is the novelty, and it is something that overturns everything, because style at this point changes, it has a sense, a road, an orientation, something to be ordered to. The sweetness has nothing to do with grace or delicacy: it is pure gratitude, it is gladness, happiness (the kind made in equal parts of joys and sorrows, not carefree cheerfulness). Strong stuff, complicated, full of shades and details to be considered and handled with care. Just the kind of thing to be discovered in Bologna, come to think of it, among people who tend never to believe anything, who do not trust unless they see twice more than Saint Thomas (the apostle) and always love to be precise, above all in matters of rules and justice. So much so that, according to Dante (but De Sanctis thinks as he does), everything is born from someone like Guido Guinizzelli.

Can you imagine anything less poetic than a notary? Guinizzelli was a notary. It seems that traditionally, having time, ink and blank spaces on the pages, they amused themselves by versifying. A real fooling around for know-it-alls, mostly, but it so happens that Guido takes a few steps further. He sets out Al cor gentil almost professionally. Without the almost: he explains scientifically how love works. He is rigorous, detailed, academic and political. He moves from feeling - though gentleness is not a feeling, it is an attitude, a decision, an approach - to very precise examples, with which he makes shades and details quite clear. He draws up a rigorous list and at the end does not forget his own trade: he imagines being contradicted and taken to court. But that is his comfort zone: although the court is the most demanding one - because the judge is He himself, God - Guido defends his own soul and the souls of everyone, even nailing the judge to his… responsibilities. With a coup de théâtre, he puts him in the dock: «it is you who sent that angel, I did nothing wrong».

But above all Guido has the courage to say “always”: Al cor gentil rempaira SEMPRE amore… There are no doubts, there can be no mistake, the rules are clear and those are the factors: there is no reason to be afraid. He is so clear-cut as to become almost a little chilly (typical of scientists and lawyers, in fact), but the input is clear: the theme is my heart, that is what needs working on.

And Dante goes to the bottom of the thing. He tells it from another angle; with the help of Saint Thomas (the other one, of the Summa), he discovers what love is and speaks of exactly that. Love is an accident in the substance. A blow, a percussion that shifts the order of reality (the celestial one!). It is a thing that strikes reality, turns it upside down, overturns it clod by clod, that creates mountains and valleys, views, journeys, bridges, leaps, roads, seas… An accident, a real blow, a thing where the rhythm goes out of the window and afterwards nothing comes back the same as before. Love… the soulmate? No. Elective affinity? No. Sacrifice for the family? No, hell no! Love shatters the substance (that is, things as God put them in place)! It shifts the essence of everything, it lights up shows of every kind of art and much more: lives, adventures, quests, journeys, risks, gambles, heroism, inventions. Boom! One blow after another, one heart after another, one beat after another, an energy is set in motion that challenges God, that carries you right before God, and it is you who speak: it is love that brought me here, I did nothing wrong. You won it yourself, now it is your home, and that embrace is many things, the embrace of God, and there is nothing servile about it. It is the embrace of a son: did you see, Dad, that I was right? And do you think I did not know you were right, my boy?

Love is not one road. It is a thousand roads that start from anywhere and arrive at that point. They are all different because they belong to whoever travels them. They also have a disconcerting feature - it is time to wake up to this! - which is the absolute moral questionableness of every path. Love is a power. It generates morality one day after another, but not with the logic of abstract respect, of rules that ride over whoever is in front of you. There is a great deal of wisdom, a great deal of good sense, but the deep complicity, the real pact, is not about the race towards destiny, not about a perfect plan of Disney inspiration or world conquest. Company is there for contemplating the wonder and running towards the mysterious origin of everything, of oneself, of one’s own heart.

This is the novelty, this is the discovery for which Dante is grateful to his father. It has the form of a journey, the style of an invitation, the dynamic of a company: Dante makes the journey with me, with you, with anyone. The idea is to see, to tell, because the aim is to live, to experience, to walk and at the end of it all to plunge body and soul into one’s own destiny. There are as many roads as there are men; they share the direction, but the steps are each person’s own. The help is in seeing; the rest is far more than intimate and unrepeatable. Precisely for this reason, for each of us every step is a wonder, because that ALWAYS is there for every one of us.
